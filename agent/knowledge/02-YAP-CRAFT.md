# YAP CRAFT — formats, frameworks, story mechanics

The analysis lenses. Everything here is corpus-cited; use these names in reports so a
reader can trace a decision back to a source.

## The format taxonomy (OREN)

| Format | What it is | Decoration rule |
|--------|------------|-----------------|
| **Standard yap** | Sitting, talking to a phone on a tripod. The base unit. | Captions recommended for almost everybody. Nothing else required. |
| **Walking yap** | Talking while moving — closer to a FaceTime with a friend. | Captions. The motion is the visual interest. |
| **Car yap** | Seated in a car, candid, not always looking at the lens. | Captions. The candour is the point. |
| **Graphic yap** | Overlays placed over the video so there is always something to look at. | One visual roughly every **2 seconds** — a 60s video is about 30 images. Vertical graphics. Framing planned so the head, the captions, and the graphic each get stable space. |
| **Full-screen overlay** | 9:16 images intermittently covering the talking head entirely. | Same 2s cadence. |

Format is declared at intake and it decides the decoration budget. VINH's guard: one or
two devices total, because doing all of them reads as a clown show.

## The five content frameworks (OREN)

1. **Strong take** — "X is the best/worst", then defend it. Forces conviction, and conversation drives videos.
2. **Strong take → education** — the take is only the hook, then it pivots immediately into teaching.
3. **Small epiphany** — the relatable noticing: "do you notice how X always…". Builds kinship: *I do that too*.
4. **Humour yap** — a funny scenario, or making fun of something.
5. **Story time** — a narrative from the speaker's life.

Strong takes, small epiphanies, and story time are the foundation for about 90% of what
works. The negative space matters as much: **"my top three favourite whatever",
listicles, and motivational speeches are generic, and generic is dead.** If the
transcript is one of those, say so in the report. No cut saves it.

## The script frameworks (OREN) — detect these in the transcript

- **Hook / Story / Point 1 / Point 2** — state the take, tell a personal anecdote that retains, then give two reasons.
- **The 8 Mile** — hook, then voice the opposition's objections yourself, disprove them, then give your reasoning. Kills objections early and retains sceptics.
- **The Four Things** — stacked mini-videos, each a small strong take, where viewers stay for the next one.

## The sub-60s story spine (HOYOS) — the story-time standard

1. **Question hook** — open with a question the viewer has to see answered.
2. **Constant progression** — every beat measurably closer to the answer.
3. **Conflict before the answer** — if everything is smooth sailing, nobody cares. A B-plot is allowed.
4. **Uncertain answer** — will it work? Keep the doubt in the cut.
5. **Fast, satisfying payoff** — delivered quickly and concisely. Imperfection is fine as long as the promise is paid.

Length discipline: story yaps bias under 60 seconds. A strong-take→education piece may
run 2–3 minutes, but only if progression never flattens.

## The engagement-device palette (VINH) — pattern breaks for the sag

Content · direct questions · props · activities · a video pattern-break · storytelling ·
analogies and metaphors. Use one when the retention curve would otherwise sag, which in
practice means the long middle of a 2–3 minute educational piece. Asks should be
strategic and trivial to answer. Maximum one or two devices per video.

## The take-quality criterion (JAKE + OREN)

A good take sounds like a social flow state: no resistance between what the speaker is
thinking and what comes out of their mouth — the joke they want to make, they don't hold
on to. Rank for flow over cleanliness, because the lit-up take with two flubs beats the
flat perfect one. Flubs are cuttable; flatness is not.

Two corollaries worth encoding as defaults: passion transmits, and **the last takes of a
session are the warmed-up ones** — especially re-recorded intros.
